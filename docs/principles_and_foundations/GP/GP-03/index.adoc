== Introduction

The following principles define the ODISC approach to system design.

* 01 Built for Today, Designed for Tomorrow
* 02 Open by Default
* 03 Solve Problems, But Not at Any Cost
* 04 Safety Before Convenience
* 05 Simplicity Through Modularity
* 06 Standardize Interfaces, Not Implementations
* 07 Primary Design Target

== 01 Built for Today, Designed for Tomorrow

The standards and architecture shall support practical, affordable
implementations today while anticipating future capabilities.

Expansion shall occur through additive improvements rather than
replacement whenever reasonably practical.

Investing in a track ecosystem is expensive, and we absolutely do not
want to make that go to waste because we didn't think about a use case
in advance. We want well thought out flexible solutions to problems, and
the ability to add to existing solutions as new problems are discovered.

We do not want to paint ourselves into a corner with any decisions, but
instead make sure that we have multiple exits available allowing future
expansion.


== 02 Open by Default

Standards define interoperability, not implementation of features.

Innovation should occur through competing implementations rather than
incompatible ecosystems.


== 03 Solve Problems, But Not at Any Cost

Technical limitations should be solved, but not at any cost.

Engineering decisions shall balance capability, simplicity, reliability,
maintainability, manufacturability, and affordability.


== 04 Safety Before Convenience

Safety-critical functions always take precedence over convenience,
performance, or feature completeness.

No capability should compromise safe operation of the racing system.

=== 05 Simplicity Through Modularity

The core functionality must be simple. Additional capabilities can be
added through modular extensions as required by tiered certifications or
club needs.

== 06 Standardize Interfaces, Not Implementations

Where mechanical interoperability is required, the standards shall define
the physical interface between components rather than the internal design
of those components.

Mechanical interfaces may define mounting points, physical envelopes,
connection locations, and other externally visible characteristics
necessary to ensure interoperability. Manufacturers remain free to
innovate within those constraints, provided their implementations remain
compatible with the standardized interface.

This approach allows track designs, infrastructure, and hardware from
multiple manufacturers to remain interchangeable while encouraging
competition through engineering innovation rather than proprietary
mechanical designs.

Example: +
The ATX computer standard defines the mechanical interface between a
computer case and a motherboard, including mounting hole locations,
expansion slot positions, connector locations, and overall dimensions.
It does not define processor architecture, power regulation, cooling
design, PCB layout, or countless other implementation details.

As a result, manufacturers compete by developing innovative products
that remain mechanically compatible with the same computer case. ODISC
adopts the same philosophy by standardizing the interfaces between
components while allowing manufacturers complete freedom in how they
implement functionality within those interfaces.

== 07 Primary Design Target

ODISC is primarily designed for racing at 1:32 scale, with support for
1:24 scale as the upper bound of the intended design target. Larger
vehicle scales are outside the primary design target, as they introduce
additional dimensional considerations such as slot size, vehicle
geometry, and component mounting requirements.

ODISC is designed primarily for routed wooden racetracks. The standards
are developed with this primary design target in mind, taking advantage
of the physical space available for mounting components while maintaining
interoperability between those components.

Nothing in this principle prevents adaptation of ODISC to other vehicle
scales or racetrack construction methods, such as molded plastic track
systems, provided those adaptations do not compromise the architecture,
interoperability, or capabilities of the primary design target.
