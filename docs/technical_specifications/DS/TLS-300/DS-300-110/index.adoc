== Introduction

This DS defines the requirements for implementing power rails that deliver 
electrical power to vehicles on the track, while also providing a 
communication medium for the Race Management System 
(xref:../../../TLS/TLS-301/index.adoc[TLS-301]) to exchange data with 
Vehicle Decoders (xref:../../../TLS/TLS-309/index.adoc[TLS-309]).

== Material Choice

ODISC recognizes two common industry approaches for power delivery and 
therefore provides implementation guidance for both.

=== Copper Tape

.Pros
* *Low cost* -- Very inexpensive to purchase and easy to budget for long 
tracks.
* *Easy installation* -- Sticks directly to the wood surface without needing a 
deep recess or routing step.
* *Simple repairs* -- Broken sections can be quickly overlaid with a new piece 
of tape.
* *Smooth surface* -- Ultra-thin profile ensures a completely flat racing 
surface.

.Cons
* *Low durability* -- Easily tears or lifts if cars deslot aggressively or 
tires spin.
* *Oxidation issues* -- Tarnishes quickly, requiring regular cleaning to 
maintain good electrical conductivity.
* *Low power capacity* -- Higher electrical resistance compared to thick wire, 
which can cause power drops on large tracks.

=== Braided Wire

.Pros
* *High durability* -- Made of woven metal that withstands years of heavy 
racing and impact.
* *Excellent conductivity* -- Delivers consistent, high-amperage power with 
minimal voltage drop.
* *Traction boost* -- Magnetic braid allows the use of traction magnets in 
cars, mimicking plastic track behavior.
* *Low maintenance* -- Resists corrosion and requires very little cleaning 
over its lifespan.

.Cons
* *High cost* -- Significantly more expensive per foot than copper tape.
* *Complex installation* -- Requires routing a special shallow shelf (recess) 
on both sides of the slot.
* *Difficult repairs* -- Glueing, recessing, and splicing damaged braid is 
labor-intensive and time-consuming.

Alternative power delivery methods are permitted by the ODISC specification. 
However, until they are incorporated into the standard, they may not be 
compatible with standardized track cutouts or mounting interfaces.

== Physical Requirements

A power rail is the conductive element installed adjacent to the slot that 
provides both electrical power and communication signals to vehicles.

The power rails shall be isolated from each other in a manner that prevents 
short circuits between rails, whether they are for the same slot or different 
rails for neighboring slots.

=== Power Rail Length Considerations

As the length of a power rail increases, electrical resistance also increases, 
resulting in greater voltage drop along the rail. To maintain consistent power 
delivery, long rails should be divided into shorter electrically connected 
segments using power taps.

NOTE: The maximum recommended rail length is currently under evaluation and 
will be defined in a future revision of this specification.

=== Power Taps and Drop Holes

At the start of each track power rail section, the track shall provide power 
tap locations to allow connection of the common power source to the rails.

==== Power Drop Hole

Power rail drop holes shall have two rectangular openings measuring 7.5 mm x 5 mm, 
with the 7.5 mm dimension aligned across the direction of track travel.

This opening supports both copper tape and braided wire power rail 
implementations.

==== Power Tap Interface

The underside of the track shall provide mounting points compatible with the 
standardized power tap footprint defined in 
xref:../DS-300-120/index.adoc[DS-300-120].

Track-side mounting provisions shall use M3 mounting hardware layout spacing 
matching a 40 mm x 10 mm array pattern.

=== Power Rail Wiring Topology (Non-Looping)

Data transmission over the DC power rails utilizes Pulse Width Modulation 
(PWM). Symmetrical propagation delays and single-source paths are critical to 
maintaining signal integrity.

Isolation Requirement::
All power rail sections must be electrically isolated from one another. This 
prevents data signal interference caused by packets entering a single 
destination terminal from multiple directions simultaneously.

Topology Clearance::
Power distribution to the racetrack layout *may* utilize a radial branching 
tree structure.

[WARNING, caption="CRITICAL"]
====
*The power delivery network shall not contain any closed electrical loops.* 

Wiring loops create parallel paths that allow data packets to arrive at a 
single point with phase variance. This propagation skew shifts data packet 
timing and corrupts signal content.
====

== Functional Requirements

=== Power Delivery and Data Transmission

The power rails shall simultaneously deliver electrical power and transmit PWM 
data packets using the same electrical conductors.

=== Track Amperage Allocation Guidelines

To calculate the maximum planned current draw for a racetrack layout, designers 
should multiply the maximum number of vehicles running simultaneously on a 
single power network hub by the peak current allocation specified for their 
target scale and ODISC Performance Level. Track designers should always use the 
maximum value in the listed range to build an appropriate engineering safety 
margin.

=== Power Distribution Wire Sizing Standards

To maintain stable track voltage under peak load, and to preserve the sharp 
square-wave rise times required for data packet integrity, power distribution 
wiring should adhere to the following distance-scaled guidelines. 

The optimal wire thickness depends on your track's maximum planned current 
draw (amperage) and the path distance from the power supply to the drop point.

Main Power Trunk Lines (Power Base to Distribution Hubs)::
All cabling running directly from the central track power base out to primary 
under-table distribution hubs or terminal barrier strips should select a size 
matching the maximum target track amperage capacity listed in the tables below.

Segment Drop Branch Lines (Distribution Hubs to Power Taps)::
All individual cabling running from an under-table distribution hub to a single 
power tap assembly module on the track should select a size matching the 
target track amperage capacity listed in the tables below.

NOTE: If multiple track segments are wired in series using serial loop-through 
connections (daisy-chaining), any conductor segment that serves more than 
one individual power tap assembly module shall be classified as a main power 
trunk line and sized based on the continuous path distance from the power supply 
to that segment.

NOTE: For racetrack infrastructures with a calculated peak current draw 
exceeding 20 A, an engineering review by a licensed electrical professional 
should be conducted prior to system installation.

[WARNING, caption="CRITICAL"]
====
*Reducing power distribution wire sizing below the recommended cross-sectional 
minimums introduces high system performance and safety risks.*

Under-sized cabling suffers from elevated linear resistance, resulting in 
severe line voltage drops under peak racing loads. This line restriction drops 
the supply voltage down enough to cause motor starvation, erratic braking, and 
unpredictable vehicle decoder brown-out resets. 

Furthermore, thin conductors attenuate high-frequency signals by rounding off 
the sharp voltage edges of the data packets, causing critical data stream 
corruption and packet loss.
====

Stranding Requirement::
All power distribution cabling shall utilize high-strand-count flexible copper 
wire. Solid-core wire is prohibited, as high-frequency data signals travel 
primarily on the outer skin of conductors (skin effect). High-strand wire 
maximizes conductive surface area and prevents signal attenuation.
